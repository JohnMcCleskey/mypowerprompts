## Concept

First-order thinking asks "what happens if I do X?" Second-order thinking asks "what happens after that, and after that?" Most people stop at the first step. Marks argues that truly superior decisions come from thinking further than the crowd. When everyone reaches the same first-order conclusion (e.g., "this drug cures disease, buy pharma stocks"), the stock is already priced in. The edge is in the second-order: what are the unintended consequences? Who wins when others win? What breaks downstream? The people who saw the 2008 crisis weren't smarter — they just followed the chain further.

## Key insight

Every decision creates a new reality, and that new reality creates new consequences. Most people plan for the world that exists now. Second-order thinkers plan for the world their decision creates.

## Practice prompt

I need to map the full consequence tree of a decision I'm considering, well beyond the obvious first-order effects.

My decision: [DESCRIBE THE DECISION OR ACTION]

Map the consequences across three orders:

FIRST ORDER — What immediately and directly happens if I take this action? (These are the obvious, intended effects.)

SECOND ORDER — What happens as a result of the first-order effects? Who else is affected? What behaviors does it change? What new problems or opportunities emerge?

THIRD ORDER — What happens as a result of the second-order effects? What long-term systemic shifts occur? What unintended consequences crystallize? What world does this create in 5 years?

Then: What decision looks different or even reverses when I consider all three orders? What am I currently optimizing for that I should actually be avoiding?

Prioritize second and third-order thinking — I already know the first-order effects.

## A small challenge

Take a decision you made in the past year. Map what you expected vs. what the second-order effects actually were. What did you miss?
