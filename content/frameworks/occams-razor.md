## Concept

Medieval philosopher William of Ockham formalized a principle that had always existed: when two explanations predict the same outcome equally well, prefer the one with fewer assumptions. "Entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity." In modern form: the simplest explanation is usually right. This doesn't mean naive — it means when you're constructing a theory or explanation, cut every element that isn't doing real explanatory work. Complex explanations are harder to falsify, harder to act on, and usually built to protect assumptions rather than reveal truth.

## Key insight

Complexity is often a signal that something is being hidden — from others or from yourself. When an explanation keeps adding exceptions and special cases to survive challenges, that's the razor saying: go back to the simpler explanation and test it first.

## Practice prompt

Apply Occam's Razor to help me cut through complexity and find the simplest, most likely explanation or solution to a problem I'm overthinking.

The problem, question, or situation: [DESCRIBE WHAT YOU'RE TRYING TO EXPLAIN OR SOLVE]

My current explanation or approach: [WHAT YOU CURRENTLY THINK OR ARE DOING]

Now apply the razor:

1. COUNT THE ASSUMPTIONS — List every assumption embedded in my current explanation. How many are there? Which ones are doing real explanatory work and which are just protecting the theory?

2. THE SIMPLER TEST — What is the simplest possible explanation that fits all the evidence? Not the most satisfying or complete explanation — the simplest one that can't be ruled out yet.

3. COMPLEXITY FLAGS — What elements of my current thinking are adding complexity without adding predictive power? Where am I creating epicycles (patches to save a bad theory)?

4. THE PARSIMONY TEST — If I had to explain this to a skeptical scientist in one sentence, what would that sentence be? Now test that sentence: does it actually explain the data, or does it immediately require exceptions?

5. ACTION IMPLICATIONS — If the simplest explanation is true, what does that change about how I'm approaching this?

Cut until it hurts. Then cut once more.
